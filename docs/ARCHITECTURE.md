# Current architecture

## Page and startup

- [index.html](../index.html) owns identity, controls, fallback text, and dialog markup. [styles.css](../styles.css) owns type, spacing, responsive layout, and paper surfaces.
- [src/app.js](../src/app.js) is the lightweight UI entry. [src/main.js](../src/main.js) initializes navigation independently and decides whether to load the deferred scene.
- [src/ui/](../src/ui/) owns hero behavior, the illustrated About estate, and Profile, Experience, and Contact dialogs. Back, Escape, dismissal, focus restoration, and the readable no-JavaScript fallback are product behavior.
- [src/scene-entry.js](../src/scene-entry.js) is the entry of the separate scene bundle, an ES module that main.js loads as a module script. The poster remains the first visual; live rendering and optional models do not block navigation.
- The scene bundle is split into content-hashed `scripts/scene.*.js` files: the entry the page names (`scene.HASH.js`), two shared chunks it imports statically (`scene.shared.HASH.js`: Three.js core and the small Pass/CopyShader base shared with the developer outline), and seven lazy chunks. `scene.developer-tools.HASH.js` contains the `?sceneDebug=1` developer camera and Three's OutlinePass; `scene.rock-build.HASH.js` contains film rock placement, fetched only on high and balanced film paths; `scene.blender-export.HASH.js` contains the editable scene conversion and GLTFExporter, fetched only when the owner calls the localhost diagnostic export action; `scene.lantern-flame.HASH.js` contains the internal flame shader, fetched while preparing a decoded supplied lantern on high or balanced. `scene.light-shafts.HASH.js` holds the film's light shafts (see Material and environment treatment), fetched only for high and balanced film scenes on WebGL2. `scene.terrain-build.HASH.js` builds the film terrain, two outer foothill bands and the twisted tree's root supports (a level root plate east of the trunk, low berms under the root ends and narrow soil banks under the resting roots) on activation, borrowing the current procedural ground until ready; an unavailable module retains that fallback. film-scene.js holds its only import and requests it when the film first activates (an earlier request beside the scene's own startup work measurably slowed the reveal). The build runs in slices of a few milliseconds, never a long task, so no frame waits for it: while the canvas is still hidden they take idle time only, so the reveal's own work goes first; while it fades in over the poster, slices of up to 12 ms run back to back, so the terrain lands inside the fade even on a throttled phone; afterwards they take idle time again. It and the mountains' build have the idle time first: while either slices, `rendering.terrainSlicing` holds a promise, shared by both, that the light shafts' own build waits on. A build cut short by disposal stops, and a terrain finished after disposal is freed. The new terrain replaces the borrowed ground only where the change cannot show mid-shot: before the reveal, during the canvas's 480 ms fade-in (timed from the `babel:reveal` mark), on a tour cut, or on any frame while the tour is not running, the rule light-shafts.js and mountain-build.js also follow. The chunk also carries the slate's puddle mirror and root shading shader text, the baked root tables and the root litter, which reach the ground only when the terrain arrives (see the puddles and the tree seating below), so none of it is in the initial scene payload. Late completion while inactive stays detached, disposal prevents late construction, and a successful change invalidates paused rendering. Terrain shares the existing ground material and draw, and the earth comparison keeps its original height field. `scene.mountain-build.HASH.js` holds the film mountains' geometry generator (see Material and environment treatment): hill-silhouette.js requests it when the film activates at high or balanced, never on low and never at scene initialization (legacy film comparisons request it too, so they keep a backdrop); it builds on the terrain's slice schedule and lands at once where the change cannot show mid-shot; later (under the fade-in, or mid-shot on a slow phone) it lands at once but fades in over the sky, 0.45 s or 1.8 s, rather than waiting for a cut; until it lands, or if the chunk fails, the film shows no ranges. Lazy modules reuse the shared Three.js instance. Shared chunks evaluate before the entry, so they may hold only third-party code: build.mjs rejects a build that would move a first-party module there. The hashed model and slate-map manifests remain string literals parsed in the entry, keeping asset revisions out of shared chunk URLs. [Blender snapshot documentation](BLENDER-SNAPSHOT.md) covers capture, returned files and renderer approximations.

The normal authored scene keeps shared terrain, sky, camera, and model activation in index.js. Procedural construction is separated into legacy-world.js and instantiated through deferred-world.js when a comparison, low-quality path, or authored-asset fallback needs it. Keep the legacy placements/materials intact when changing those modules. URL comparison precedence is centralized in scene-modes.js.

Scene subsystems share applyQuality, resize, update, and dispose lifecycle hooks where relevant. Derived resources must restore borrowed model/material state before disposal; quality changes and failed or stale asset loads must preserve a usable fallback.

The replacement lantern is an independently optional architecture channel (`includeLantern`), enabled on the authored scene's high and balanced startup tiers. It neither blocks the tower/tree nor participates in camera readiness. Legacy comparisons and low keep their procedural lantern; download, validation, or texture failure retains the tree's procedural stand-in without constructing legacy scenery. Its content-hashed URLs live in the scene manifest only; the UI's early prefetch still requests only tower and tree. Asset leases retain its source maps through staging and release aborted or stale decoded resources using the same ownership path as the other architecture channels.

`lantern.js` preserves the owner's supplied PBR materials and masked emission without the tower/tree material grade. It bakes node transforms into cloned geometry, bottom-centres the footprint, and normalizes its height to 2.48 authoring units; the existing `tree-lantern` parent gives 1.98 world units under default prop scaling and retains the ground anchor. Scene extras `lantern.luminousCenter` identify the emitter centre in the GLB's coordinate space. The reversible mount detaches the procedural geometry, reuses `tree-lantern-light`, and moves it to that centre without adding lights. A ready asset commits before reveal, on a running tour's next cut, or on the next still frame when the tour is paused. Each commit refreshes camera measurements for both lantern views, redraws shadows and the held scene, holds adaptive sampling, and warms shaders. On restoration the procedural geometry and original light position return before the source asset lease is released.

Staging remains synchronous and returns its cleanup immediately while `lantern-flame.js` loads. The mount retains the asset lease and waits for preparation before committing; a stale or disposed stage cannot acquire the effect. Import or preparation failure still prepares the supplied lantern with its static PBR glow. The effect (flame v3, the owner's recommended realistic flame of 2026-09-27; program key suffix `|lantern-flame-v3`) extends only cloned MeshStandardMaterials with an emissive map. `lanternFire()` casts the eye ray onto the vertical plane through the wick that faces the eye and draws an analytic flame profile there (`FLAME`: foot 0.49, 0.30 tall and 0.047 half-width in the lantern's 2.48 authoring units): a blue cup, a darker vapour cone over a charred wick with an ember, a yellow-white core, a yellow body and an orange-red tip, with a slow wander, a flutter near the tip and the draught's lean. It is a stylised slice, not a volume. The globe is clear glass: the emission mask (`smoothstep(.08, .4)` of the emissive-map sample) is combined with a spatial gate on the globe's shell (`lanternBulb()`), which removes the mask's UV-island seams, and under it the diffuse colour goes to zero, roughness to 0.1 and metalness to 0, with the geometric normal for crisp moon glints. Its emission is the flame, a halo hugging it and a faint fill, a soft glare, plus a warm Fresnel rim; `lanternBloom` (1 where a bloom pass runs) trims the rim from 1.6 to 1.5 times and the glare by a quarter, never removing them: at this flame's size the high tier's bloom adds almost nothing, and without them the globe nearly vanished there (review 2026-09-28). A frame may pass it as `bloom`; otherwise the renderer's shadow map stands in, since quality.js gives shadows and bloom to the same tiers (high). So that the cage shows through, the globe's triangles (every corner inside the shell, `SHELL` and `FLAME.globe`: the supplied GLB's 266 emission-mask triangles plus two slivers under the finial) move to the end of the index as draw group 1, drawn by one glass clone per hooked material (`<material> glass`, key suffix `|lantern-glass`). The clone takes a mask of 1 and opaque alpha and adds its light over 92% of what lies behind it, with no refraction, keeping the subject's depth-layer code in alpha; the split adds one draw and no geometry, texture or light. After `lights_fragment_end` the cage's own share of the practical light is rescaled by its distance from the flame (clamped to 0.4 to 1.25 times; on the cage itself about 0.4 to 0.72, least dimmed at flame height), so the frames at flame height stay brightest; the light is matched in `pointLights[]` by its exact position (`lanternLight`, updated each frame from the point light among the lantern root's siblings that sits nearest the flame). One draught, `lanternDraught(t)` (a calm breath plus one gust per 7.5 s cell at a hashed moment, strength and heading), returns glow, height and lean: it shapes the flame and scales the glass light (the lantern root carries its uniform as `userData.lanternFlicker`, which the puddle mirror reads), and lantern.js passes its glow to the tree, which drives the existing point light by it, clamped to 0.88–1.12 of the quality- and film-derived base it caches; the factor is noncumulative and resets to 1 on restoration. The clock advances by frame deltas of at most 0.1 s and freezes for `motionPaused` or reduced motion, and the draught is pure in time, so it freezes with it. Disposal restores the index order, the groups and each mesh's own material, then the shader hooks, and frees only the glass clones.

Adaptive quality changes only per-frame cost: pixel ratio, shadows, post-processing, counts and leaves. index.js passes the startup tier as the `assetTier` quality context, which the model, stone, ground, film ground (slate or earth), grass and brick controllers use instead of the profile tier, so a step never blanks or re-downloads models or terrain maps. Frame intervals are sampled only after the scene is revealed, and not for three seconds after a resize, resume, dialog release or asset commit (quality.js `sampleRevealed()` and `holdSampling()`). A revealed scene never steps its visuals down to low: the governor's low step keeps the current profile and lowers only the pixel ratio to 1, the composer resolution every tier had before device-pixel targets, and recovering from it restores the tier's cap. Samples are display intervals, not render cost, so recovery counts only frames that keep up with a 60 Hz or faster display: the window's 10th percentile is at most 17.5 ms and the average within 10% of it, which stays under the 20 ms downgrade line. A capped or GPU-bound steady rate never counts as headroom. A 120 Hz panel held to a GPU-bound 60 fps still looks like a 60 Hz display with headroom; the backoff bounds that case. The wait doubles after each recovery that is followed by another downgrade. After two such oscillations, recovery stops. On a display cadence, a render that keeps it reads as one 60 Hz frame: a cadence slower than 60 fps leaves out the vsyncs it waits by design, and a faster one reports its real interval, so a late render counts as it would on a 60 Hz display. Where the browser compiles shaders in parallel, a step links its programs when it is sampled (rendering.js `prepareQuality()`); otherwise they link on the cut frame. While the tour runs, a step applies on the next cut, where the dissolve's kept frame hides its one-off work (runtime.js `createDeferredQualityStep()`); sampling pauses while it waits, and a dissolve's capture, cut and first blended frames are not sampled.

runtime.js renders only frames a visitor can see, on an even cadence: renders land on every nth vsync of the estimated refresh rate near 60 Hz, so 90 Hz desktops draw every frame and 144 Hz ones 72 fps, while touch screens stay at or below 60 (45 fps at 90 Hz); until a rate is adopted, 60 fps caps it. Before the reveal, the transparent canvas redraws only when a status, model commit, resize or scroll invalidates it; the frame that sees readiness shows the canvas and resumes animation. An open dialog holds the tour at once, and 450ms later, after the dim overlay has faded in, a named scheduler hold stops rendering until the last dialog closes (`createPanelHold()`). A resize draws one frame behind the dialog, then the hold returns. A dissolve in progress when the tour is held shows its incoming shot. Scripted review can call `scene.setVisitorPaused()`, read back with `scene.isVisitorPaused()` (the footer's Pause scene button was retired on 2026-09-24): it pauses the tour and, from the reveal onward, holds rendering through a second named hold (`createVisitorHold()`) that composes with the dialog's. A resize, context restore or content change (model, map, shader, font) draws one still frame; scroll does not. The sceneDebug developer camera lifts the hold while it runs and restores the held frame on exit. A `scene.visitorPausedPreference` pre-set by a script applies at the reveal and keeps the first revealed frame; the UI clears any legacy stored pause and sets it to false. Without the legacy world, the sun's shadow map redraws only after a shot focus, quality, resize, model or treatment change. The legacy world keeps per-frame shadows for its animated clutter. If the procedural fallback fails to build, the scene stops and leaves the static poster.

rendering.js also checks the underlying WebGL context before entering the composer. This covers physical context loss before the browser's queued loss event reaches Three.js, while preserving the existing restoration and disposal lifecycle.

The composer's targets and passes follow the canvas in device pixels. The pixel ratio is capped at 1.5 on high, 1.25 on balanced and on every touch-primary device, and 1 on low. The renderer's own antialiasing is off because it would reach only the final full-screen pass. On WebGL2 with float color buffers, high draws the scene into one four-sample target, which Three resolves once per frame, and copies it into the composer's read buffer. Bloom and grading then draw into single-sample ping-pong targets, which carry no depth buffer while the scene has its own. A tier change reallocates the scene target. Star sprites and solar prominences are sized in CSS pixels, bloom keeps its CSS-pixel resolution, and the ink contour samples one CSS pixel apart, so they keep the look reviewed at DPR 1.

### Start-up cost

The page opens two WebGL contexts: the probe in [shared/webgl-probe.js](../src/shared/webgl-probe.js) and the renderer. The probe caches the texture-size and anisotropy limits that the quality tier uses. index.js passes those limits to quality.js, which opens its own probe context only when they are unknown. All three places request `powerPreference: "default"`, because the ambient scene does not need a discrete GPU.

rendering.js `compileShaders()` links the scene's programs through `renderer.compileAsync` and KHR_parallel_shader_compile, so they do not stall a draw. It compiles against a composer target, because program keys differ for off-screen output. It always settles. It returns false at once when compileAsync or the extension is unavailable, because the draw would link anyway. It also returns false when compileAsync rejects or after two seconds, and the next draw then links whatever is left, as before. index.js runs one warm-up after initialization and one after each model commit, each in a separate task (runtime.js `createShaderWarmup()`). Until the canvas is first shown, nothing draws while a warm-up is pending, and the reveal waits for it. After the reveal, a committed model that replaces nothing visible stays hidden until its programs are ready. A swap for a visible procedural stand-in draws at once, as before. The tree carries two lights and program keys count lights, so every compile briefly shows the subjects still waiting. Shadow-depth and post-processing programs still link on first use.

The live scene's entry and two static shared chunks are requested together. Unaided, the browser would request shared chunks only after downloading and parsing the entry, one round trip later; under throttling that delayed the model-free low tier's reveal by about 6% in the earlier two-request layout (see [OPERATIONS](../OPERATIONS.md)). build.mjs defines the entry's statically imported chunks in the UI bundle (`__BABEL_SCENE_MODULE_PRELOADS__`), and main.js appends a `<link rel="modulepreload">` for each just before the entry's module script; the entry's imports reuse those downloads. The developer and Blender export chunks are never preloaded or requested by default visitors. main.js keeps its load, error and retry handling for the module script: a failed load (including a failed chunk or preload) rejects and removes the script so a later call can append it again, reusing the existing preload link, although a browser may reuse a failed module fetch for the same URL within that page.

The flame chunk is also never preloaded: its request starts only after a supplied lantern has decoded and entered preparation. Neither is the light-shafts chunk: index.js requests it while initializing a high or balanced film scene on WebGL2 and the reveal never waits for it. Its maps build in idle slices, after the film terrain's own, and its programs link on a detached stand-in only once the canvas shows, so they never compete with the reveal's own warm-up; it commits during the canvas's 480 ms fade-in if it is ready by then; a light still building when its own shot is on screen (The watch after a fast reveal) fades in over a second once its programs are linked, and otherwise waits for the next tour cut. Neither the tower/tree readiness path nor a low or legacy scene waits for it.

Model downloads start beside the scene bundle rather than after the scene's first frame. After main.js chooses the live scene and requests the bundle, it selects the startup tier with quality.js from the cached probe limits. It then requests that tier's complete tower and tree at low fetch priority. The build defines the full hashed model manifest in the scene bundle and only each tier's tower and tree URLs in the UI bundle. A hidden page, which draws no frame, makes the request only if it is shown while the bundle still loads; once the scene has initialized, its first frame requests the models itself. The in-flight responses are shared on `site.scene.prefetched`, keyed by URL. architecture-assets.js uses a response only when its URL matches, and at most once. Validation, the byte budget, abort and fallback behave as before. It makes its own request if the early one failed or was unsuccessful. When the live selection starts, the controller aborts the requests it did not take, such as another tier's; teardown, a failed bundle and a failed initialization also abort them. Static-poster paths, the low tier, unknown texture limits and `architecture=` comparisons make no early request.

On high and balanced film paths, initialization fills the procedural ground with only a flat 4×4 preview, because the film ground maps (the default slate pair, or the `ground=earth` comparison's earth maps) replace it, and textures.js paints the full-size canvases in a task of its own right after. The reveal, a legacy fallback that cycles the film ground off and on, and a film-map fallback therefore show the painted ground while maps load; a fallback before that task paints at once (`ensureProcedural()`). Comparisons, the low tier and `ground=procedural` or `ground=desert` paint in full at start-up, as before.

[perf-marks.js](../src/scene/perf-marks.js) records User Timing entries that Lighthouse and the Performance panel list under `babel:`. The marks are `scene-request`, which [main.js](../src/main.js) records when it appends the scene module script, and `scene-entry`, the start of the entry chunk's evaluation. Under ES module rules the shared Three.js chunk that the entry imports has already evaluated by `scene-entry`, so `scene-eval` excludes Three.js evaluation and is not comparable with records from the former single-bundle build; the span from `scene-request` to `scene-entry` covers both downloads and Three.js evaluation. The measures are `scene-eval`, `init`, `first-frame` (the first frame that draws), `glb-parse:<role>`, `assembly:tower`, `assembly:tree`, `shaders:scene`, `shaders:tower`, `shaders:tree` and `ground-paint` (full paints only). `reveal` marks the first frame that shows the canvas.

## Material and environment treatment

architecture.js preserves supplied geometry, UVs and textures while preparing runtime materials. The complete tower is the supplied timber fire lookout: a color map and a baked tangent-space normal map, with the bake's tangents stored in the GLB, so GLTFLoader keeps the map's sign and `MATERIAL_PROFILES.tower` applies it at full strength (normalScale 1). It has no roughness map, so its 0.90 scalar roughness is applied directly rather than remapped toward 1. Its moonlight grade (`FILM_GRADES.tower`) treats the whole structure as weathered wood; the height band that once warmed the stone tower's timber balcony is gone. The tree keeps its normal and metallic/roughness maps, with roughness mapped into 0.84–0.97. The tree's cool crown fill light (`tree-fill-light`, unshadowed) reaches 24 units in film instead of its prop-scaled 37.8, so it still models the bark and the lantern cap but no longer floods the lantern clearing; its range is restored when film is off. Rendering uses existing fill and hemisphere lights for a restrained cool shadow lift; the sun and lantern remain the warm accents. No light or shadow pass is added.

light-shafts.js (the lazy `scene.light-shafts.HASH.js`) draws the owner's hybrid light shafts (2026-09-25; visible rays chosen 2026-09-26): warm star rays only in The watch, where the star is in frame behind the cabin; cool moonbeams along the moon key light (`rendering.lights.sun`, taken by reference) in Threshold, Gallery detail, Masonry study, Portrait and Close-up; none in Lantern study or Root and lantern (`SHAFT_SHOTS`, keyed on the directed shot's name). index.js imports it during initialization for high and balanced film scenes on WebGL2 that are not legacy comparisons, never for low, WebGL1 (its shaders take derivatives and its back map has two channels; three r160 falls back to WebGL1 where WebGL2 is missing) or the static posters, and registers the subsystem it returns; a failed import leaves the scene as it is, and one that resolves after disposal registers nothing. The subsystem checks the film, the two subjects (`complete-meshy-tower`, `meshy-tree`) and their materials each frame: a subject not found yet is looked for on every frame drawn (a still, reduced-motion frame draws only a few, and the tree arrives late), a known one when the film changes, it leaves the root or changes material, and every 30th frame; a change disposes that subject's light, restoring its own program first, and rebuilds it. On a shot with no treatment the update stops after those checks. A quality step changes only its march length and, for the star, its streaks (sparser on balanced, with darker gaps and 1.5 times the light in each, so a phone's smaller tower keeps distinct rays rather than a warm veil), taken from the profile being applied (the star 12 steps on high and 6 on balanced, the moon 9 and 6; a subject's own march takes half of them for the star and a third for the moon's crown): its hooked programs carry both shadow states, which `rendering.prepareQuality()` links before the step lands. A step on the cut into a subject not yet shown, whose programs must then link again, clears the previous shot's light with the cut, and the new light fades in mid-hold once they are linked. Building takes one subject at a time, the tower first (its star, then its moon), after a 32 px blue-noise tile for the march offsets, in idle slices budgeted at 5 ms (2 ms when the idle wait timed out) that yield by the work done; while the film terrain's own build slices (`rendering.terrainSlicing`, terrain-build.js), it waits, as the terrain can land only under the fade-in or on a cut while the star can fade in mid-hold. The moonbeams also wait for the terrain's height (`filmScene.ready`). For each subject and light the CPU rasterizes the subject's own triangles into a light-space map with mip levels (the star's a perspective from it, 192 px, aimed into the tower so it holds the cabin and the whole lattice; the moon's orthographic, tower 160 px and tree 192): R the first hit, G the light's window, B the light the subject lets through (one minus its coverage, lightly blurred) inside its closed silhouette (its gaps closed over a few texels by a dilation, then an erosion that leaves the edge just inside the outline; for the tower's moon a little outside it, so beams graze past the solid cabin), A the front of that closed silhouette (none outside it); a second, two-channel map holds its back: R that closed silhouette's last hit (spread four texels past its edge, each taking its nearest valid neighbours' nearest back, so linear filtering never blends toward "no depth" and draws a one-texel ring of light over the open sky) and G the subject's own last hit (none in its gaps; for the star, more than four texels inside the closed silhouette's edge, blended toward that closed back). The air is lit only past that front, by the light the subject lets through there, so the light maps give a lit volume shaped by the subject's closed silhouette and its transmittance; the visible streaks are a stylised screen-space ray pattern laid on that volume (below), not the gaps alone: with so few steps the transmittance must be read soft, and only part of the structure comes from it. B is read at a softer mip level the further past the front, and at least at the level of the march's own step across the map, so a ray's edge softens toward the eye and few steps leave no grain; the front itself softens by the step. Where a treatment asks (Portrait's crown), part of B (tie) is read two levels sharper, so the crown's own gaps shape part of the shafts, and each streak fades in over a few units past the front (rise, varied per streak), so none starts bluntly in open sky. The star's window is a soft round one over the tower, fullest through the cabin and upper lattice; the moon's G holds large broken-cloud patches over the whole subject (the light on the bark and timber, for the tower kept near the cabin), and the break in the cloud where the air takes it goes into B. Once the canvas shows it links just the new programs, the subject's hooked program on a detached stand-in and the box's, through `renderer.compileAsync()` with the scene's lights and against the composer target, as `rendering.compileShaders()` does, without preparing every other material again. Program keys count the scene's lights, so until a subject first commits, a change in its light set (the tree brings two) links its programs again; after that the scene's own warm-ups include them. Each treatment has one box, turned about the vertical to follow its light, holding the subject and where its light runs on past it as far as its reach or the subject's foot, its level floor just above the film terrain under it (the terrain support height that `filmScene.ready` resolves to, or the analytic ground on the earth comparison). The box's back faces, drawn with the depth test on, march the view ray behind every opaque surface; the subject's own material marches the air in front of it (not the tower under the moon: over the whole tower in Threshold that march costs more than its faint streaks show, so the box's shafts carry it) and adds the treatment's light where it lands (a composed `onBeforeCompile` hook with the program key suffix `|light-shafts-N`, N the moon's slot among shadow casters; on high the moon's light on the tower also takes its real shadow map, four taps read at a named level, while the tree's is shaded by its own light map and wraps round the limbs toward the eye), under a soft shoulder that keeps a lit surface's direct light below about 0.6, so timber never reads as under a spotlight; the rim catch fades where the surface turns faster than a pixel, so twigs take no isolated glints. Every texture read in the march names its level (`textureLod`), so no gradient sits inside the uniform-gated branches and ANGLE on Direct3D keeps them real branches: a hooked subject with no treatment on screen costs what an unhooked one does. ## Camera and composition

The default is a fitted directed camera, opening on **The watch**, then touring seven tower/tree shots, each held 6 to 9 seconds and joined by a 1-second dissolve staggered by depth. Masonry study is retained for explicit comparison URLs but excluded from the tour. It is not governed by the old fixed orbit-radius guidance. Shot regions, subject bounds, terrain clearance, portrait overrides, and the DOM space occupied by identity/navigation determine the composition. The tower remains the world anchor; measure visual changes from the selected camera and responsive safe area.

The tower shots follow the timber lookout, placed at height 39 with its ladder and gallery opening on local +Z and the π/4 yaw. The watch frames its upper half (region 0.5–1, azimuth −9, height 0.68) so the gable stays clear of the fixed sun on desktop and the sun stays inside the top edge on landscape phones; phones keep its earlier portrait framing, and landscape phones (and any viewport under 500 px tall) take a lower, further-round variant (height 0.55, azimuth −12) that keeps the snowy range out from behind the intro. `resolveDirectedShot()` resolves a shot's `portrait` variant for narrow or portrait viewports and its `landscape` variant for short ones, each built once. Threshold is the ladder arriving through the gap in the gallery railing (0.64–0.93 from azimuth 82; portrait phones keep 0.62–0.90). Masonry study keeps its comparison name as a structure study of a corner leg and its X-braced lattice (0.30–0.60, azimuth −20). Gallery detail runs from the gallery floor to the eave (0.72–0.94, azimuth −100; portrait phones keep −90), across the corner between the cabin's local +X plank panel and its back gable; facing that panel (azimuth −30) centred the balanced tier's faceted reduction patch, which phones load by default. These three shots keep the sun wholly out of frame in the desktop, ultrawide, portrait-phone and landscape-phone layouts that test/solar-framing.test.mjs checks, which allows another tower shot to show the sun only whole, clear of the name and unoccluded. Nearer the ladder face, Threshold put the sun behind the name on landscape phones; Masonry study at azimuth 10 put it behind the name on portrait phones, hidden only by the gallery floor. The end-of-hold push-in set four of these values: The watch turns to −9 so the sun clears the eave corner, Threshold's region reaches 0.93 so the gable apex keeps headroom under the top edge, Gallery detail turns to −100 so its plank panel stays clear of the intro's last words on desktop and landscape phones, and Masonry study's fitting margin of 0.87 keeps its far corner leg off the right frame edge. The ladder foot (8.4–9.0 out on +Z) rests on the flat terrace.

## Assets and build

Editable runtime assets live in this checkout's images and fonts folders. The separate workspace Assets root contains original artwork, preparation history, and QA evidence; it is not published wholesale.

The scene builds before the UI, which names both shared chunks for their preloads, so a shared-chunk change (a Three.js update) also yields a new UI bundle URL. Before esbuild minifies the scene, tools/shader-compact.mjs strips indentation, comments and spaces beside punctuation from the template literals that hold GLSL, keeping newlines around preprocessor lines and template expressions; esbuild otherwise leaves template text verbatim.
